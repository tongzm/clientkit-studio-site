How to Calculate a Reseller Break-Even Price

A reseller break-even price is the sale price that covers the costs included in your calculation, leaving neither a profit nor a loss on that item. It gives you a reference point before you source, list, or discount inventory. It is not automatically a good selling price: you still need to consider your desired margin, market demand, and the fees that apply to your account.

This guide starts with a simple percentage-fee example, then adds fixed sale costs and overhead. All amounts and the 10% fee below are illustrative, not a claim about any marketplace's current rates.

The reseller break-even price formula

Start with your cost basis (C): the item cost plus inbound shipping and other acquisition costs. Let r be the percentage selling fee written as a decimal, so 10% becomes 0.10. If that fee is charged only on the sale price and there are no other costs in the calculation, use:

Break-even price = C ÷ (1 − r)

You divide rather than subtract 10% of the cost because the fee is calculated from the sale price. At the resulting price, the part left after the percentage fee equals your cost basis.

For a more complete item-level calculation, add fixed sale costs and any overhead you deliberately allocate to the item:

Break-even price = (C + F + O) ÷ (1 − r)

  • F is fixed per-sale costs, such as a fixed payment fee, shipping you pay, and other direct sale costs. If the buyer pays some shipping, use your net shipping cost rather than counting the full amount twice.
  • O is an optional, documented overhead allocation. It is not required for a direct-cost break-even price.

This formula assumes the percentage fee applies only to the item sale price. If fees also apply to buyer shipping, taxes, or another amount, model that full fee base instead of forcing the numbers into this simplified equation.

Worked example: a $19.50 cost basis

Suppose your cost basis is $19.50, already including the item, inbound shipping, and other acquisition costs. Use a fictional 10% fee and assume no fixed sale costs or allocated overhead.

  1. Convert the fee to a decimal: 10% ÷ 100 = 0.10.
  2. Find the portion left after the fee: 1 − 0.10 = 0.90.
  3. Divide the cost basis by 0.90: $19.50 ÷ 0.90 = $21.666….
  4. Round the asking price to cents: $21.67. At that rounded price, a 10% estimate is about $2.17, leaving approximately $19.50.

The formula result is $21.666…, not exactly $21.67. Marketplace rounding and the actual fee base can create a small difference, so check the final numbers using your platform's rules.

A price below this calculated break-even point would not recover the included costs under these assumptions. A price above it does not tell you whether the margin is adequate until you calculate the profit and margin separately.

Margin versus markup: use the right denominator

Margin uses sale price as its denominator. Markup uses cost. Keeping those denominators separate prevents an unexpectedly low margin.

  • Margin = (sale price − cost) ÷ sale price
  • Markup = (sale price − cost) ÷ cost

For a simple target-margin calculation before percentage selling fees, rearrange the margin formula:

Target-margin price = C ÷ (1 − m)

Here, m is the desired margin as a decimal. For a 30% target margin on the same $19.50 cost basis:

$19.50 ÷ (1 − 0.30) = $19.50 ÷ 0.70 = $27.857… ≈ $27.86

Ignoring selling fees for this comparison, the markup at $27.86 is ($27.86 − $19.50) ÷ $19.50 = 42.87%, while the margin is approximately 30%. A 30% markup would therefore not produce a 30% margin.

If your 30% target means margin after the 10% sale-price fee, use (C + F + O) ÷ (1 − r − m). With only the $19.50 cost basis, that becomes $19.50 ÷ (1 − 0.10 − 0.30) = $32.50. The $27.86 calculation and the $32.50 calculation answer different questions; label which costs your target margin includes.

Where overhead belongs

Overhead includes business costs that are not necessarily tied to one sale, such as software, storage, or general packaging stock. It differs from the acquisition costs and direct sale costs already in C and F.

You can review profit at two levels:

  • Item contribution: sale revenue minus item cost basis and direct selling costs. This helps compare items and pricing decisions.
  • Business profit after overhead: total item contributions minus overhead for the period. This shows what the business keeps after those broader expenses.

You may allocate overhead to an item as O, but write down the method and keep it consistent. An arbitrary allocation can make products look more or less profitable without improving the underlying records. Even when item-level break-even excludes overhead, your after-sale business review should still subtract it.

Common break-even calculation mistakes

  • Subtracting the fee from cost. For a sale-price-based fee, divide by 1 − r instead.
  • Confusing markup with margin. Check whether the denominator is cost or sale price.
  • Leaving out acquisition costs. Include inbound shipping and other direct costs in the cost basis once, not twice.
  • Using a generic fee as your actual fee. Verify the rate, fixed charges, and fee base for your own marketplace and account.
  • Ignoring net shipping and fixed sale costs. Add them through F when they belong in the item calculation.
  • Forgetting overhead after the sale. A positive item result does not guarantee positive business profit after overhead.
  • Rounding too early. Keep precision through the calculation and round the final price appropriately.

Frequently asked questions

Does break-even always include overhead?

No. A direct-cost break-even price can exclude overhead, but then it is not a whole-business break-even figure. Add a documented allocation through O or review overhead separately at the business level.

What if my fee applies to buyer shipping too?

Calculate the fee on the full amount to which it applies. The simple C ÷ (1 − r) formula only covers a fee on the item sale price, so include shipping-related fees in your model.

Why does my calculated break-even sale show a few cents of profit or loss?

Rounding the price or fee to cents can cause a small difference. A changed fee, shipping amount, or omitted cost can do the same. Recheck the actual transaction rather than relying only on the estimate.

Is break-even the price I should always charge?

No. It is a floor only for the costs and assumptions included. Use demand, condition, competition, desired profit, and your actual fee structure to evaluate the final asking price.
